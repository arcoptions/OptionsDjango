{# ---- 2. what to expect -------------------------------------------------- #}
What this list does and does not tell you

The rule is three filters on yesterday's close: average daily swing above {{ atr_cut_pct|floatformat:0 }}% of price, price above ₹{{ min_close|floatformat:0 }}, and turnover above ₹{{ min_turnover_cr|floatformat:0 }} cr a day. The first is the whole signal; the other two only remove names you cannot get in and out of cleanly. Ordering is by turnover, so the cheapest to trade sit at the top.

It finds movement, not direction. These names are far more likely than average to make a 5% move — {{ exp_move|floatformat:1 }}% against {{ base_move|floatformat:1 }}% — but roughly half those moves are down ({{ exp_up|floatformat:1 }}% up). Every directional rule tested either vanished or inverted once real option prices were applied. The chart read is yours; this is the watchlist it operates on.

Read that middle number carefully. If you buy a call on every name here and read the direction no better than a coin, you lose — the premium is the toll and {{ exp_up|floatformat:1 }}% does not cover it. The list is only worth trading if your chart read is genuinely better than {{ hurdle|floatformat:1 }}% correct. Puts are excluded because the same measurement puts their break-even above 100%: no read is good enough.
This hurdle was corrected upward on 19 Aug 2026, from 56.8%. The option history is built on nearest-expiry contracts, so a five-session hold that crossed an expiry was priced against the next month's option at the same strike — one carrying a fresh month of time value the trade never paid for. Those trades showed a profit when the stock fell, which is impossible, and they were flattering the toll. The two panels below are unaffected: they score the stocks, not the options.
