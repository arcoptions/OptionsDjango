The finding
{{ tested|add:"-2" }} of the {{ tested }} buy-side strategies tested lose money after the spread. One clears it — and it was not on my list.

Fourteen entry signals × five exits, run against real fixed-strike option paths with the tick charged both ways. Momentum, breakouts, OI surges, IV rank, moneyness bands — on every signal with a real sample the answer is 0.75–0.89× per rupee committed. Then following the data rather than my signal ideas landed on expiry-day at-the-money calls with a trailing stop: 1.041× across 21,693 entries. Section 7 is that trade and everything wrong with it.

Your observation was correct. UNIONBANK 190 CE really did go 0.70 to 5.00, and {{ run_count }} contract-cycles here ran 3× or more off their own low. The moves are real. What is not there is a way to know in advance which option is about to be one of them — section 5 is the finding that generalises past anything I happened to test.

0. Read this before the rest — what this data cannot answer

I found this after writing everything below, and it changes how three sections should be read. Every option bar in the cache was pulled at Dhan's relative_strike='ATM', front month only. The feed re-centres on the money continuously, so a strike is only present while the stock is standing on it — 86.7% of bars sit within 1% of the strike, and 100% of the {{ run_count }} big runs fall inside ±2%.

UNIONBANK is the clean illustration: the stock ranged ₹106.87 to ₹205.14 over the eighteen months, and the 190 CE exists in this cache for exactly 121 bars — every one of them with spot between 188.76 and 191.24. The moment spot left that band, the contract left the data.

Stands, untouched
  • The base rate — correct, for ATM options.
  • The exit finding — same paths either way.
  • The expiry-day result — it required |moneyness| ≤ 1.5%, which is exactly where this data lives. The limitation cannot touch it.
  • The Chartink work — equity only.
Withdrawn or narrowed
  • “OTM 2–6% + momentum” was never really tested — only 0.68% of call bars sit in that band, by accidental drift. Those 305 trades are a scrap sample.
  • “Median moneyness zero” was circular — nothing else was in the dataset. Section 6 is rewritten; the days-to-expiry half survives.
  • “No entry filter can exist” is now a claim about ATM options only.

So the honest position on your UNIONBANK thesis: buying a 190 CE at ₹0.70 with spot near ₹175 is a deep-OTM lottery ticket, and this dataset contains no such trade. I cannot tell you it works and I cannot tell you it fails. ATM±1, ±2, ±3 is downloading now (research/fetch_otm.py, live progress in research/fetch_otm.log) — with two caveats, both mine to own:

  • It is slow. 29,484 windows at ~13/minute is roughly 36 hours, not overnight — the first-pass ATM job is still running and competing for the same rate limit, and ~13% of requests come back 504 and get retried. Offsets are fetched nearest-first, so it degrades gracefully: ATM±1 across the whole universe lands in the first few hours and on its own roughly doubles moneyness coverage, enough for a first real answer on the 2–6% band.
  • Even finished, it will not reach your example. On a 2.5-point strike ladder ATM±3 is only ~±4% on a ₹190 name. A ₹0.70 premium there means spot was ~8% away — outside what the rolling feed serves at any offset. That needs the ladder feed (real contract security ids), a separate pull, and it is the honest next step for your specific thesis.

1. What the base rate actually is

Before any strategy, the ground truth. A stock option bought at a random bar and held two sessions is worth 0.77× when you sell it. It touches 2× at some point 0.62% of the time. Not 62%, not 6% — six-tenths of one percent. This is the number every rule below has to beat, and it is the reason the account bled: the default outcome of buying an option is losing about a fifth of the premium every two days.

3. The exit mattered more than any entry

Read the table above by exit rather than by signal and a pattern appears that no entry rule produced. Holding for two sessions returns 0.773×. The identical entries with a trailing stop 30% off the peak return 0.882×. That is eleven paise on the rupee recovered by changing nothing but when you sell — larger than the spread between the best and worst entry signal in the whole study. It is the same lesson the NIFTY work landed on: the exit is the lever.

4. The Chartink scans

Both scans were measured from their own backtest exports, so there is no risk of my reading of the rule differing from Chartink's. Two corrections change the answer completely. First, Chartink stamps a trigger with the candle's start, not its close — both exports contain 09:15 triggers and no candle has finished at the opening bell. Entering at the stamped bar means buying with the breakout candle's own move already in hand. 5. Were the big winners recognisable beforehand? No.

This is the most important test in the study, because it does not depend on my choice of signals. Take the 3,203 trades that doubled, take the 459,622 that did not, and compare what was observable at the moment of entry. Open interest: identical. Volume surge: identical. Implied vol: near identical. Stock momentum in the prior hour: the doublers were marginally weaker. A stock breakout was in progress for 2.3% of doublers and 4.4% of everything else — the breakout pointed the wrong way.

Only two things separated them, and both are arithmetic rather than edge. Premium: doublers started at ₹5.25 against ₹28.75, because a cheap option needs fewer rupees to double. Days to expiry: doublers had a median of 3 against 15. Which brings us to the real shape of the thing.

If the winners cannot be told apart from the losers at entry, no entry filter can exist — not the fourteen tried here, and not the fifteenth. Scope (section 0): this is a statement about at-the-money options. Within that population it is solid — 3,203 doublers against 459,622 non-doublers is a large, clean comparison. It is not a statement about OTM options, whose entry characteristics I have not observed.
6. What the 2–3× moves really are

Here are the {{ run_count }} biggest runs in the data, largest first. Look at the DTE at low column. Median days-to-expiry at the low: 2, and the distribution is bimodal — 205 of the {{ run_count }} (59%) were within three days of expiry, 124 were freshly-rolled front-month contracts at 31–34 days. Because DTE ranged freely from 0 to 34, this half of the finding is real: the largest single cluster of 3×+ runs starts on or within days of expiry day.

The Moneyness column needs the section 0 caveat. Every one of these {{ run_count }} sits within ±2% of the money — but so does all the data, so that is the boundary of the cache, not a discovered property of big movers. I originally read “median moneyness zero” as a finding. It was circular, and it is withdrawn.

For the near-expiry 59%, the mechanism is not in doubt: at-the-money options with hours left, where all remaining value is gamma and a 1% move in the stock is a 10× move in the option. That is the machine that produced the UNIONBANK chart you remembered. Whether the same multiples are available further out of the money is exactly what this data cannot say, and exactly what the download running now is for.

7. The one thing that cleared break-even — and what is wrong with it

Section 6 pointed somewhere specific, so it got tested on its own terms: expiry-day (DTE 0–1) at-the-money calls, exited on a 30% trail off the peak. 21,693 entries across 23 expiry sessions and 87 symbols. Net expectancy 1.041× — the only rule in the study to clear 1.0 on a serious sample.

I expected a settlement artefact. The 15:15 slot alone printed 1.42×, F&O runs to 15:39 on quotes nobody can fill, and that is exactly how a fake edge looks. So it got the hostile treatment instead of a victory lap:

Drop the best 5 of 23 expiry sessions 0.966× fails

So it is neither an artefact nor a one-half fluke. It is also not a reliable edge, and the reason is in the rest of the numbers: median trade 0.847×, win rate 26.1%, 9 of 23 expiry sessions profitable, day-clustered t = 1.11.

That is a positive-expectancy lottery: the entire mean lives in the top 1% of trades. Real, and tradeable — but the opposite of what it feels like to trade. You lose on three trades in four and on three expiry days in five, and the money arrives in rare violent bursts. Sizing it like a normal trade guarantees the account is gone before the tail shows up. And 23 expiry sessions cannot distinguish a real fat tail from a lucky one. The correct next step is a small fixed-size forward test on every expiry, with no discretion — not a reallocation.

This is also the honest answer to the UNIONBANK question. The 2–3× moves are real, they are catchable, and the structure that produces them is expiry-day gamma. What they are not is easy — the same structure that produced 31× on SRF produces a 0.85× median and a 74% loss rate, and nothing observable at entry tells you which one you are in.
8. Where the edge sits structurally

Every buyer's 0.77× is somebody's 1.23×. Mirroring all 462,825 trades to the sell side: the seller collects +19.4% per rupee of premium sold and wins 79.8% of the time. That is not a discovery, it is the same measurement read from the other end — but it is where the money in this dataset went.

It is also not a recommendation to sell naked. The worst 1% of those short trades average −474% of premium collected: one gap wipes out a hundred good weeks, and margin on a naked stock-option short is roughly 15% of contract value, so return on capital is nothing like return on premium. The honest version of this finding is defined-risk spreads — keep the decay, cap the tail.

That has now been built and tested. It does not work, and section 9 is as much about why the first version of it said the opposite as about the verdict.

9. Defined-risk spreads — tested, and how the answer flipped

Sell the ATM call, buy the ATM+1: collect a credit, cap the loss at the strike gap minus the credit. Section 8 called this the next thing worth building, and the cache already had what it needed — 434,903 ATM/ATM+1 call pairs quoted at the same instant, 189 symbols, 366 sessions. {% if spreads.five_day %}It returns {{ spreads.five_day.imputed|floatformat:1 }}% on the capital at risk at a five-day hold,{% else %}It loses money at every horizon,{% endif %} and loses in {{ spreads.months_down }} of {{ spreads.months }} months.

For most of a day it did not. The near-expiry version showed +18% of risk with every expiry cycle profitable, and it had already survived a non-overlap test, a cost decomposition and a check that it was not a disguised direction bet. All of it was one bug, and that bug is a property of this data rather than of this strategy — so it is the part of this section worth reading.

The bug — exits go missing only in the losing direction

relative_strike is ATM-relative. Both legs are correctly pinned as fixed contracts at entry — but a pinned strike only stays in the cache while spot stays near it. So an exit that fails to price is not missing at random: it is missing precisely because the market ran away from the strike, and for a short call spread, spot running up is the losing direction. Dropping unpriceable exits deletes the losers, and only the losers.

Method, so you can attack it
  • Fixed strike, one expiry cycle. Dhan's rolling feed is ATM-relative: ask for "ATM" and each bar hands back whatever strike was at the money at that moment, changing every five bars or so. Buying "ATM" Monday and selling "ATM" Friday is not a trade — it swaps contracts underneath you and manufactures returns. Every series here is rebuilt as a fixed strike inside a single monthly cycle.
  • Stale quotes removed by self-contradiction. A freshly-rolled contract that has not traded is quoted at a nominal five paise: TRENT 4700 CALL showed ₹0.05 with spot 4673 and thirty days left, when it was plainly worth about ₹150. Those quotes invent thousand-baggers two bars later. Dhan publishes its own IV on the same bar, so each option is priced from that IV and quotes disagreeing by more than 3× either way are dropped. The filter keeps 91.4% of bars and cut the measured 2× rate from 7.64% to 0.88%.
  • Entry is the next bar's open. Never the signal bar's close.
  • Missing exits are repaired, not dropped. The same ATM-relative feed means a pinned strike stops being quoted once spot walks away from it — so an exit that will not price is missing because the market moved, which is a direction. Dropping those trades silently deletes one tail; section 9 is what that costs. Any test that follows a fixed strike forward values a vanished leg at max(spot − strike, 0) instead.
  • Corporate actions are cut on the strike, not on the move. That repair has its own fault: a split re-bases spot but not the recorded strike, so max(spot − strike, 0) subtracts two different price scales. It is rare and it dominates — 1.17% of five-day exits carried 70% of the measured loss. The cut is taken on how far the strike sits from spot (a scale check, direction-neutral) and never on how far spot moved, which would delete the adverse outcomes and manufacture a positive. Threshold 0.30, chosen off a plateau: the result barely moves between 0.50 and 0.15.
  • Stops win ties. When a 15-minute bar's high and low would both trigger, the stop is taken — the bar cannot say which came first, and assuming the good one is how backtests lie.
  • Costs at the tick. One full 5-paise tick each way plus 0.28% turnover tax. On a 70-paisa option that tick is 7% a side, which is exactly why the cheap options that double most often still lose the most money.
  • Significance clustered by day. Overlapping entries on one name in one hour are one bet wearing many hats.
